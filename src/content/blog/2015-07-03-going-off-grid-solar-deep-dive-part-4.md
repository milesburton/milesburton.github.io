<p>Off-Grid set-ups can genuinely feel liberating. You are able to generate and store your own power for use at any time of the day and aren&#8217;t dependant on the reliability of the National Grid – only the weather! This means you could – in theory – set-up a tent in the middle of a field and retain the luxuries that electricity bring, tin-foil hats optional.</p>
<p>Unlike GT systems, you really only need to concern yourself with <a href="http://www.planningportal.gov.uk/buildingregulations/approveddocuments/partp" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Part P of the building regs</a>. There are some exceptions, such as running new conduits around a house, but assuming you’re wiring up an temporary building it isn&#8217;t usually applicable.</p>
<h4>Powering your project</h4>
<p>In outbuilding or shed projects you’re more interested in average load rather than base load. Chances are you’ll have a power draw that spikes at a few points in the day – for example, mowing the lawn. This is important because you need to estimate the capacity of the battery you buy.</p>
<p>For example, a low grade 300 Watt <a href="http://www.civicsolar.com/resource/pure-sine-vs-modified-sine-wave-inverters" target="_blank" rel="noopener">modified sine wave inverter</a> uses about 0.6 amps idling – if hypothetically you wanted to run a desk fan for 6 hours a day we would need to consider the power draw during those 6 hours. In this case a fan consumes roughly 1Ah. Using a bit of bar-mat maths we can say <i>((1.6 * 6) + (0.6 * 18) * 1.2) = 22.56Ah</i>. So assuming your battery isn&#8217;t being charged, you’ll need at least 23 amps to last a day– realistically you&#8217;d want to increase the 1.2 fudge factor so you avoid running the battery below 20% to avoid damage. While there are <a href="http://www.energymatters.com.au/components/batteries/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">more factors</a> such as power draw over time and battery temperature that impact battery capacity, this is a reasonable rule of thumb.</p>
<p>If however, you can afford to buy a higher capacity battery – always go for the largest you think is reasonable. It’s generally better to have a bit of breathing room when the weather gets bad or you need more capacity later on down the line than to randomly run out of power at the worst time.</p>
<p><i>And this leads us neatly to&#8230;</i></p>
<h5>Batteries</h5>
<p>When you first start experimenting with Solar I suggest picking up a cheap <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_cycle_battery" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Deep-Cycle battery</a>. Also known as Leisure batteries as they are often used in boats and camper vans. Thankfully, as they are so widely used this makes them readily available at most automotive shops like <a href="http://www.halfords.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Halfords</a>.</p>
<h6>AGM or Standard Deep-Cycle? ..Come again?</h6>
<p>The reason we use deep cycle batteries rather than a more traditional car batteries is they emphasise long continuous current draw opposed to a momentary, but significant, power spike like starting a car. While models vary, you can generally drain the 60-80% of the charge without damaging the battery (<i>damaging the battery usually means reducing it&#8217;s capacity</i>).</p>
<p>The alternative to deep-cycle are <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VRLA_battery#AGM_.28Absorbed_glass_mat.29" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Absorbed Glass Mat (AGM)</a> batteries. Technically they are actually a form of deep-cycle battery but they have a few key differences. Generally they are quite a bit more expensive compared to their deep-cycle, &#8220;flooded-cell&#8221; brother. For that extra cost you gain more power density at the same size – in other words, you can have more capacity in the same space physical space with AGMs. Durability also plays a factor in AGMs, providing you do not discharge below 60%, it should outlast a standard deep cycle battery. For more information <a href="http://www.wholesalesolar.com/solar-information/deep-cycle-battery-info" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Wholesale Solar</a> have covered the differences quite well.</p>
<h6>Building up &amp; Capacity Planning</h6>
<p>When you first start out you’ll probably start small, and want to build up. This is neither a problem for the solar array nor batteries.</p>
<p>Let&#8217;s say you buy a new deep-cycle battery (don’t mix and match battery types!) you could, at a high level, connect your batteries in parallel with a suitable fuse between each positive connection. This would increase your battery capacity but not your voltage, which is generally the preferred approach. <a href="http://caravanchronicles.com/guides/how-to-connect-two-batteries-in-parallel-part-2/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Caravan Chronicles</a> provides an excellent write up of the various approaches to wiring your batteries together.</p>
<h5>Charge Controllers</h5>
<p>Charge controllers are a rather interesting beast of the Off-Grid solar system. There are many different variants ranging from total junk to apocalyptically expensive so I’ll do my best to break it down.</p>
<p>The primary reason we use charge controllers, instead of simply wiring the panel to the battery, is to avoid a potentially explosive battery overcharge, and to optimize the electrical flow to the battery. Secondly they also provide a rather handy regulated 12vDC output which can power your devices. In the majority of controllers you gain metrics about the amperage generated and consumed by your system.</p>
<p>Charge controllers typically come in two core variants of charging circuit, pulse width modulation (PWM) and multi point path tracking (MPPT). A mouthful indeed, yet they are very important to your systems efficiency.</p>
<h6>Pulse Width Modulation</h6>
<p>At a high level PWM is a very fast on/off switch. Depending on how long it is on* determines the average voltage output (sort of).</p>
<p>A solar panel is constantly changing voltage depending on the sunlight exposure and temperature. Unfortunately your battery wants a consistent voltage to avoid damage.</p>
<p>The PWM controller is constantly adjusting the modulation based on the solar panel output and, ideally, temperature (more on this later). When all is well, the battery receives a voltage which is more or less constant*</p>
<p><i>* Once again this is a <a href="http://e-bluelight.com/article-345-Pulse+Width+Modulation+Charge+Controllers+Explained.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">more complex than I&#8217;m</a> describing, but you can effectively treat it as accurate</i></p>
<p><a href="//solarcraft.net/articles/comparing-pwm-and-mppt-charge-controllers/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Solar Craft</a> covered the differences between the two in some depth</p>
<h5>Alternative Current (AC) or Direct Current (DC)?</h5>
<p>DC is native to solar systems system. Your solar panels, charge controller and battery all use DC that means you do not incur the loss of converting DC to AC – and that loss can be quite substantial. Naturally the caveat is you are now limited to devices which accept 12vDC from your charge controller. All is not lost though, if you have a look around your home most if not all devices actually accept 12vDC.</p>
<p>That said, running AC within your system can be really useful. It enables you to run all your normal household devices from your own power generating system, however, it does come at a cost.</p>
<p>Converting DC to AC requires an inverter. The inverters job is to step up the voltage and, <em><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_wave">attempt</a></em>, to produce a sine wave.  Avoiding too much detail, there are a couple of factors at play here. High voltage over distance loses less power than it&#8217;s low voltage equivalent &#8211; there was a rather <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_Currents">spectacular fight</a> in the late 1880&#8217;s on this topic and, I promise you, it&#8217;s far more entertaining than me. Oh, and it<a href="https://www.physicsforums.com/threads/why-dc-is-said-to-be-more-dangerous-than-ac.563362/"> saves lives</a>! Suffice to say <em>(you&#8217;ve not left me in a dormant tab now have you?)</em> we need to turn the native DC into AC to supply your household gadgets.</p>
<p>The cost I previously mentioned goes back to our old friend efficiency. Each time we manipulate the flowing electrons there is a loss, actually there&#8217;s a great metaphor which should help you understand. Let&#8217;s say you need to get from Brighton Marina to London Victoria. A map will probably tell you you&#8217;ll need to walk, take a bus and &#8211; if it arrives on time &#8211; a train. Each mode of transport travels a different speeds <em>(current)</em> and can hold different quantities of people <em>(voltage)</em>. Unfortunately each time you change there&#8217;s a little delay, or not enough people can get aboard so they have to wait. All this adds up and can seriously reduce the efficiency of your journey.</p>
<h6>Is there a way to improve that efficiency?</h6>
<p>An interesting question. Inverters come in all shapes and sizes, but there are two key versions you&#8217;ll come across, &#8220;Modified Sine Wave&#8221; and &#8220;Pure Sine Wave&#8221;.  The former is generally very cheap to make, it uses PWM (above) to &#8220;fake&#8221; a Sine Wave. It turns off and on at a particular interval &#8211; say 50Hz &#8211; which, sort of, looks like a Sine Wave. Unfortunately few devices, particularly sensitive devices, like computer PSUs don&#8217;t appreciate this. The reason is actually fairly simple, modified sine wave devices are flicking on and off very quickly &#8211; what would happen if you did the same to your house-hold light? I&#8217;m guessing it&#8217;ll hold up for a little while and then go <em><strong>BANG</strong></em>. This aside, these devices are muuuch cheaper.</p>
<p>Pure sine wave however, employees some sort of smoothing (or is entirely native). Rather than an on/off motion it produces, well, a smooth wave &#8211; just like when you&#8217;re at the beach. The current draw of the devices remains constant as the current delivered to the device is equally constant. When the current is constant the device doesn&#8217;t suddenly have to suck extra power to make up for the absence of power. Analogy time! Go take away a milk-shake from a toddler* and give it back, over and over again &#8211; I&#8217;m betting they&#8217;ll draw far more milky goodness than they would if you just left it with them 🙂</p>
<p><em>* don&#8217;t do this! It&#8217;s far more fun to try this with your partner. Make them a nice smoothie &#8211; honestly, for science</em></p>
<h6>So which inverter do I buy?</h6>
<p>Inverters will come with two rating, constant and peak wattage. If you want to run your 800 Watt lawn mower you&#8217;ll need to consider it&#8217;s &#8220;starting&#8221; draw.</p>
<p>&#8230;Damn these analogies. Let&#8217;s imagine it&#8217;s your lazy sunday, you&#8217;ve been working hard all week and you&#8217;ve been looking forward to a nice, long, nothing in bed. If I suddenly shock you and said, <em>&#8220;to work!&#8221; </em>you&#8217;d be less than impressed. If you&#8217;re like me a nice cup of tea would be required, possibly three &#8211; and a toast, let&#8217;s not forget toast. It&#8217;s all a bit shocking getting back in gear ready for an unexpected day. However, if it were 11am on a Wednesday and I tapped you on the shoulder and said, &#8220;Can you help me with this&#8221; &#8211; you&#8217;d probably be just fine. This is very much the same with most devices. They have to counteract <strong>Inertia</strong>. In our world this means a significant power draw for a short amount of time. <em>[Ed: You should remind them of the differences between a Deep Cycle (leisure)  and a Short Cycle (car) battery &#8211; nah they&#8217;re a bright lot! They know all about batteries]</em></p>
<p>Earlier we discussed Base-Load, now we need to take it a step further and think about the devices we want to power. If you want to power a laptop then it&#8217;s starting current is still fairly modest. However, if you want to fire up some power-tools (I&#8217;m English, but still &#8216;Muurrrica) they are powered by motors. <em>Bloody analogies. </em>When you generate power little electrons shoot towards negative<em> (<a href="http://www.allaboutcircuits.com/textbook/direct-current/chpt-1/conventional-versus-electron-flow/">you didn&#8217;t know that?</a>)</em> when they encounter a motor which isn&#8217;t moving they build up and, in simple terms, draw more companions to help push. In our pink and squidgy world this is seen as a huge rise in Amps (the flow of electricity). Eventually, assuming no magical smoke appears, the motor will begin turning and those little fellows will run unimpeded leaving the motor to run as fast as they naturally go (current).</p>
<p>So when you choose an inverter, consider not only whether it&#8217;s Pure or Modified but it&#8217;s peak current. However, don&#8217;t go too overboard with your inverter as you&#8217;ll use power purely to keep it running.</p>
<h6>Why not both?</h6>
<p>Actually, there’s nothing stopping you from running both. You can complement your circuit with an inverter, which can be enabled using a switch giving you the best of both worlds. I&#8217;ve heard this complaint before, people seem to believe you either have an inverter or go pure DC, this is totally inaccurate.</p>
<p>My recommendation is consider your requirements and size everything appropriately. It&#8217;s not quite in the scope of this blog, but you can actually get devices which shut off. In my example, I have a very inefficient inverter that runs at .6 amps. I could set my device to cut off if it remains at .6 for more than an allotted time. Not too much lost 🙂</p>
