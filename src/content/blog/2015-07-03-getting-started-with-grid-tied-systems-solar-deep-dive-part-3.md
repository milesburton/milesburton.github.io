<p>Grid-Tied systems have an awful lot of potential, and frankly I&#8217;m surprised they aren&#8217;t more popular. While the FIT may have <a href="https://www.ofgem.gov.uk/environmental-programmes/feed-tariff-fit-scheme/tariff-tables" target="_blank" rel="noopener">dropped recently</a> the payback period is still quite good. I suspect one of the key reasons is the over regulation of the MCS scheme and subsequent market reaction to it; In short GT installations are relatively expensive because of the ‘few’ qualified engineers who can actually carry out the work.</p>
<p>Typically a 3.9KW installation costs around £6,000-7,500, of that the majority of the price is labour costs. Now unless you happen to live in a tower or under the sea it just doesn&#8217;t cost that much to perform the installation. With a full 4KW “kit” costing around £2,200 retail that’s quite the mark-up!</p>
<p>With this in mind, it becomes just a touch more attractive to look at a DIY solution – even if you still have the grid hook-up carried out by a MCS installer. For example, you can purchase and <a href="http://www.planningportal.gov.uk/permission/commonprojects/solarpanels/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">mount your own panels</a> without planning permission and you could even prepare the <a href="http://www.planningportal.gov.uk/buildingregulations/approveddocuments/partp" target="_blank" rel="noopener">majority of the circuit</a>.</p>
<h4>How do Grid Tied Inverters work?</h4>
<p>To gain a better understanding of what is required, let’s look at the key components. The first of which is the Grid-Tie Inverter. It is designed to convert the power generated by your panels into a form which can be hooked to your incoming home power supply.</p>
<p>The tricky part is the National Grid <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_frequency" target="_blank" rel="noopener">“cycles” at 50Hz</a>. If you attempted to use automotive inverter, like the ones used power a laptop in your car, which does not sync you would likely cause a black-out in your area as the various safety systems would assume an overload occurred!</p>
<p>Naturally this is rather dangerous, particularly for the fellow who has to fix the problem while you’re feeding power into their equipment! For these reasons a GT Inverter performs the following tasks:</p>
<ul>
<li>Steps up and converts the DC solar panel voltage to 220~ volts AC</li>
<li>Matches the 50Hz AC cycle of the National Grid</li>
<li>Protects electricians by shutting down power if an outage is detected on the National Grid</li>
</ul>
<h5>Safety &amp; the Microgeneration Certification Scheme</h5>
<p>Unlike a standard pure sine wave inverter, a GT inverter supplements your existing house power and exports any unused generation to the National Grid. In theory this is a great way to save money, but unfortunately you need to be an Microgeneration Certification Scheme (<a href="http://www.microgenerationcertification.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">MCS</a>) certified at the time of writing, and for good reason. This is currently a legal requirement in my region and you can be fined a substantial amount (not including any external damaged to life or property) – that said, check with your <a href="http://www2.nationalgrid.com/uk/Our-company/electricity/Distribution-Network-Operator-Companies/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">local supplier</a></p>
<p>If you’ve ever searched for GT Inverters on YouTube you’ll find a worrying number of installs that connect directly to a standard plug socket. Despite many GT inverters like the Mastervolt Soladin 600 carrying <a href="http://www.energynetworks.org/electricity/engineering/distributed-generation.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">G83/2 certification</a> many provide a kettle lead for direction connection to a consumer socket. This is a violation of <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BS_7671">British Standard 7671</a> and provides no protection from shorts by your consumer-unit.</p>
<h4>Getting set-up</h4>
<p>Now we&#8217;ve got the doom and gloom out the way a low-end GT system is actually relatively simple to carry out safely. As I mentioned earlier, you can carry out the majority of the install yourself all you need to do is source the components. Understandably prices vary wildly depending on the size of your array but hopefully I can give you a rough idea.</p>
<p>Typically GT only mildly differs from an Off-Grid system, and is generally simpler schematic. At a minimum you’d want:</p>
<ul>
<li>[£250-550] GT Inverter suitably sized for your array</li>
<li>[£3] One feed in fuse between the AC Disconnect and your Grid Power Supply</li>
<li>[£20] AC Disconnect</li>
<li>[£40] DC Disconnect</li>
<li>[£7] MC4 inline fuses (1 per panel)</li>
<li>[£10] MC4 splitter if you have more than one panel</li>
<li>[£10] MC4 combiner if you have more than one panel</li>
<li>[£3] One source fuse between the GT Inverter and solar panels</li>
<li>[£0.80/Watt] MCS certified Solar Panel(s) – The price drops off substantially if you buy in bulk</li>
<li>[£???] An appropriate Solar Panel mount which allows for airflow under the panel – Dependant on panels and mounting location.</li>
<li>[£???] Appropriately sized cable for your setup – <a href="http://www.solar-wind.co.uk/cable-sizing-DC-cables.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Thicker the better</a> in almost all situations</li>
</ul>
<p>A GT circuit is quite simple. Your Solar Array is connected together using MC4 cables. Each panel will have a fuse, likely 10 amp for most 250Watt panels. All panels are then combined together to DC Disconnect (a switch) via a fuse. The DC Disconnect is then connected to the input on your GT-Inverter. The output of the GT Inverter is connected to an AC Disconnect, via another fuse. Finally the AC Disconnect is wired to a dedicated 16Amp circuit on your consumer unit.</p>
<p>You’re probably wondering why I am not providing specific panel sizes, cables and fusing – this is quite intentional. Solar configurations can vary so wildly based on the space available, base-load, inverter specification and mounting locations. My recommendation is begin with the amount of space you have available to mount panels and their location. Armed with this information you can investigate the type of panels you need and a suitable inverter.</p>
<h4>Certifying your installation</h4>
<p>If you carried out the majority of the installation, I’d recommend leaving the output from your AC Disconnect to the grid unconnected. At this point forward the MCS electrician will finish and test the circuit. It is worth checking how much they will let you perform before you go ahead.</p>
<p>When a MCS certified engineer installs your setup he will either wire a connect to a dedicated circuit on your consumer box, or using an appropriate junction just before. He will also check you have a suitable energy meter which will let you take advantage of the government Feed-In-Tariff (<a href="http://www.energysavingtrust.org.uk/domestic/feed-tariff-scheme" target="_blank" rel="noopener">FIT</a>).</p>
<p>In theory you can have your design “signed-off” by an MCS Certified Installer but be sure to check before you even consider it.</p>
