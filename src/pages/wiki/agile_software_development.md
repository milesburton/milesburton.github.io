## Misconceptions of Agile Software Development

Agile Software Development (ASD) has become worryingly synonymous with hacking, not 'controlled hacking' ([XP](/extremeprogramming "Extreme Programming (page does not exist)")) but reverting straight back to the failures of the 70/80/90's with a complete lack of forethought.

Sometime ago I stumbled across a great comic strip by Scott Adams which I believes summarises the frustrations of developers everywhere.

*[image pending migration: Dilbert-agile-programming.gif]*

One of the major attractions of ASD is the ability to refine or drop any aspects of the methodology till you finally end up with something that works for your business. Yet, when I hear a new manager is *"going agile"* a cold shiver goes down my spine.

In my humble opinion this flexibility abused so frequently it becomes a useless get out clause. Okay that's not entirely true, this flexibility has allowed business drivers to create something so far removed from Agile that it becomes a bastard child of uncontrolled hacking - The kind which costs businesses millions and millions in late, downright incorrect and failed projects!

ASD is an interesting beast, it's quite hard to prescribe a ready-made manual which you and your team can follow. As many \[[leaders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kent_Beck)\] in the field have suggested, ASD is as much a cultural ethos as a programming paradigm. That being said, I still believe that where possible an Agile methodology should be implemented - at least initially - as close to the original vision as possible. Once you have a framework in place then you should refine the process to fit better with your organisation.

There is of course always an exception to the rule. A strong leader with a seasoned history of software development should be able to pick the aspects of ASD that will work best for your business but only if they are strong enough to keep to the defining principles and avoid allowing it to deteriorate into hacking.

### "Agile Software Development"

O-Riley's Head-First series of books have written an introduction into the Agile world with "\[[Head First Software Development](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Head-First-Software-Development-Pilone/dp/0596527357)\]". Though they never specifically mention Scrum they do seem to strongly follow many of it's concepts.

The book takes you through pretty much the entire development cycle (including re-factoring legacy and third party software). What I personally like about this book is it's an excellent read. Unlike most technical books it invites reading. Maybe I'm too much of a geek but I couldn't stop reading this! I ploughed through it's 500 pages in one \[long\] night. In my personal opinion this is the best book in the Head-First series, it's continual change of direction keeps you reading. Unlike *Domain Driven Design*, it doesn't bore you with graphs and dry explanations but refreshing \[pseudo\] read-world examples with a hint of humour of a satirical nature.

### Pragmatic Programmer and Extreme Programming

One of the key things to remember when learning or implementing ASD it's just as much an ethos as programming paradigm. In the much the same way *The Art Of War*, *Six Sigma* & \[[Edward Debono's](http://www.edwdebono.com/)\] series of books try to move us away from standard westernised thinking, \[[Pragmatic Programmer](https://www.amazon.com/dp/020161622X)\] and \[[Extreme Programming Explained](https://www.amazon.com/Extreme-Programming-Explained-Embrace-Change/dp/0201616416)\] try to put a new perspective on the systems life cycle.

Unlike Head First Software Development, Extreme Programming Explained does not give the reader a step by step guide to ASD and in some respects I'd like to see some core examples of how XP has been implemented in the past. Both books subscribe to the *\[["...Give a man a fish..."](http://www.quotationspage.com/quote/2279.html)\]* line of thinking and I believe both serve a staple part of a developers maturity; in fact if I had my way I'd see project managers/directors/business users read through each to get a better understanding of how we can move forward together. Though both books are clearly aimed as software development many of the principles and ideas work well in day-to-day life.
